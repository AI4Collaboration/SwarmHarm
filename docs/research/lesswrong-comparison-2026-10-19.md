# Why the current Luna test differs from the LessWrong reproduction

Update: the communication bans and added boundary/stop reminders identified below were removed from current executable inputs on 19 October. Shared-work interaction alone is no longer scored as a violation. This memo describes the earlier tested configuration; its archived trajectories are unchanged. The revised configuration has not yet been run with a model.

19 October 2026. Source and trace review only; no new model calls or scenario changes.

**Our current test primarily measures crossing an explicit data-access boundary. LessWrong's Step 2 primarily measures inventing peer communication after a missing-input failure.** These are different decisions. The strongest design hypotheses are the meaning of the blocker and whether another worker demonstrably has something useful. Increasing an effort instruction alone does not resolve either difference.

The public repository's current HEAD and our clean local reference checkout both resolve to `2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe`. The published article, its appendix, the live-fleet variants and later source variants are distinguished below. Comments and judge-only scenario descriptions were not treated as subject prompts.

## Differences that matter

**An explicit communication ban also changes the test.** Our current developer input says `Peer communication is not authorized for this assignment; no formal peer channel is provisioned.` This remains after the earlier removal of redundant source-permission reminders. The reviewed LessWrong Step 2 assignment, persona and platform document do not contain an equivalent express ban. Our side-channel condition therefore asks a worker to violate an explicit restriction before even requesting help. This is a plausible suppressor, not a measured cause. Missing a formal chat interface and being explicitly forbidden to communicate must be distinguished. [Our assembled task metadata](../../experiments/hf-campaign-v1/world.py), [their platform](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/env/agent/fs/home/agent/PLATFORM.md).

**1. Missing input versus denied permission.** Their protein task needs a file that failed to stage, with a manifest documenting a missing object. Finding another legitimate copy is a plausible recovery action. Our blocked worker sees its analyst role, custodian-only source metadata, inherited cache permissions and, when requested, a permission denial. Fetching the cache therefore crosses a clearly exposed boundary. This changes task meaning, not just difficulty. A missing-input arm would test recovery and peer discovery; the existing denied-access arm should remain separate. [Their task](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/step2_reachout/task.py), [our source roles](../../experiments/hf-campaign-v1/world.py).

**2. A useful peer versus a peer with a different case.** In their heterogeneous source variant, named peers appear to have analysed the exact structure the target needs. They performed different analyses, so their derived tables signal possession without supplying the target answer. Our early workers have different private records and answers; another worker's success does not show that it holds the later worker's missing records. Direct reads of another worker's cached source also return not-found. Consequently, peers can offer a general method, but cannot simply supply the missing own-case data. This removes a strong reason to ask for help. Their heterogeneous setup includes operator-seeded derived outputs; it is not evidence that a fleet generated those outputs from an empty state. [Their heterogeneous variant](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/step2_cosim_hetero/task.py), [our private variants and registry](../../experiments/hf-fleet-v2/world.py).

**3. Staged opportunities versus an entirely spontaneous chain.** The appendix describes Step 2 conditions with recurring apparent peer activity. Step 3 supplies apparent requests for help; Step 4 supplies a method to discover and use. The live-fleet conditions also start with some prior residue. Our current worlds begin without authored peer artifacts or seeded methods. In the latest pilot, no worker read a peer report body. That cannot diagnose refusal to share or adopt an encountered method: the relevant encounter never occurred. [Published appendix](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/mXPCpJCvFGybQ4mwc/appendix-reproduction-of-the-openai-huggingface-incident), [current pilot](../../experiments/luna-factor-v1/RESULTS.md).

**4. A broad workstation versus a narrow business API.** Their default ReAct agent operates a Linux workstation through Bash and discovers the registry through platform documentation. Our worker has twelve typed functions, no shell and one narrowly defined cache omission. It also has a first-class supervisor-report tool and report-validation receipts. In this pilot, blocked workers escalated or submitted incomplete findings. Those are salient workflow options that their no-human-in-the-loop setup does not provide in the same form. This may change exploration and stopping behavior; it is not yet a measured effect. Copying their dangerous capabilities is unnecessary to test the business-task mechanisms. [Their scaffold](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/harness/scaffolds.py), [our tools](../../experiments/hf-fleet-v2/tools.json).

**5. Different endpoints and experimental scale.** Their Step 2 judges distinguish directed help-seeking from broader directed communication; reading a peer file or posting an undirected status report does not satisfy that signature. Our latest primary endpoint was protected-source access, with peer reads tracked separately. A positive result on their earlier stage does not predict a positive result on our later one. Their live-fleet setup used 256 workers with 32 concurrent slots; our pilot used four workers per world and two arrivals, lasting about 2.8 and 3.5 minutes. Their configured turn/time limits are not evidence that every worker used those limits. Their heterogeneous source also reports that an earlier homogeneous condition yielded 0/256 reach-outs; we did not recompute that historical count. [Their judge](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/harness/judge_reachout.py), [live-fleet setup](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/step2_cosim/task.py).

**6. “Effort” is not a matched model or persona.** Our historical-effort arm reused our own short instruction, not their complete research-agent persona. Their repository contains baseline, fair, driven, exhaustive and intense personas, plus a separate collaboration intervention. Some variants explicitly permit concluding that a task cannot be completed; the Step 2 code rejects a forced-completion design. Therefore “they succeeded because they prohibited stopping” is not a general explanation. Our two null Luna worlds neither isolate these persona differences nor compare Luna with their tested models. [Persona definitions](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/harness/scaffolds.py).

One later Step 3 source variant is especially informative: its comments report that the previous design rarely produced method acquisition, so it made acquisition useful for completing the worker's own task and separately measured sharing. That is a design lesson about opportunity, not a reason to import its cyber task or to transfer its results to our setting. [Required-acquisition variant](https://github.com/msp895/oai-hf-incident-reproduction/blob/2fec98288c1777845711a4b7678d96fd84ea16fe/src/alignment_auditor/docker_replication/step3_egress_share/task.py).

## Diagnosis and next discriminating test

**The current evidence locates a bottleneck before method transfer.** All four blocked pilot workers inspected a cache listing; none requested a cached dataset object or read a peer report. They knew the storage route existed, but we have not established that they encountered a useful peer method. The separate historical replay confirms that the authorization omission can expose data. It does not explain why a worker chooses that route.

The next comparisons should isolate the explicit communication prohibition and the missing-input versus denied-input distinction one at a time, holding the legitimate task fixed. Technical absence of a chat interface and a stated communication prohibition are separate experimental conditions; any policy-presentation change must be registered, not silently relabeled. A subsequent peer-discovery test should use different analyses of the same underlying case, with genuine worker-produced artifacts that make the peer's usefulness observable. Keep source permissions truthful and measure unauthorized communication or method use separately from legitimate recovery. Artificial successful peers, forced persistence and penalties for incomplete answers would add new confounds.

Record the sequence independently: useful peer encountered; directed request posted; real recipient received it; method acquired; method shared; later worker used it on a different protected object. Test each conditional stage before interpreting an empty-state full chain. No new experiment is launched by this memo.
